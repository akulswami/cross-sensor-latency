## Related Work

Characterizing the delay between a physical event and a system's response draws on several distinct literatures. We organize prior work into the analytical framing of end-to-end latency, the empirical practice of edge-AI timing benchmarking, the treatment of temporal misalignment in multi-sensor fusion, and the measurement-science principles that motivate external instrumentation. We then state precisely what remains uncharacterized: the sense-stage latency term and, critically, the mechanistically distinct ways it arises across sensing modalities.

### End-to-end latency in real-time systems

The real-time systems community has developed a rigorous vocabulary for end-to-end latency along cause-effect chains, distinguishing *data age* (how old the data underlying an actuation is, in the worst case) from *reaction time* (how quickly a system can respond to a stimulus), and establishing that end-to-end latency decomposes compositionally across chain segments [Günzel et al., TECS 2024; ECRTS 2023]. This framework underpins timing certification in automotive and avionics domains. It is, however, an *analytical* framework: worst-case bounds are derived from assumed task execution times, and the analysis conventionally begins at the point where sensor data enters the software pipeline — implicitly treating sensor acquisition as instantaneous. The interval between a physical stimulus and the availability of the first digitized sensor output at the chain head is outside the model. Our work supplies, by direct measurement, exactly the quantity this framework assumes away, for three sensing modalities.

### Edge-AI and in-sensor inference timing

A large body of work benchmarks inference latency on resource-constrained hardware. Recent comparative studies measure forward-pass execution time across heterogeneous edge and in-sensor processors using cycle-accurate profiling [Capogrosso et al., I2MTC 2026], and in-sensor ML benchmarks extend standardized suites to sensors with embedded compute [Aymone & Pau, Information 2024]. These works are valuable for ranking compute platforms, but their measured quantity is the *inference* stage: the timer starts once input data resides in processor memory, and latency is attributed to clock frequency, memory hierarchy, and accelerator utilization. The sense stage — transduction, conditioning, conversion, and readout that precede the first software-visible sample — is explicitly outside this measurement. Capogrosso et al., for instance, define latency as the end-to-end time to execute a single forward pass and discuss the analog front-end only qualitatively in an energy context; sensor acquisition latency is neither measured nor compared across modalities. Conventional edge-AI timing therefore characterizes how fast a correct result is *computed*, not when sensing makes a result *possible*.

### Temporal misalignment in multi-sensor fusion

Autonomous-vehicle perception has studied *sensor staleness*: because cameras, LiDAR, and radar capture at different rates and through different processing pipelines, their outputs are temporally offset, degrading fused state estimates. State-of-the-art approaches compensate algorithmically — for example, per-point timestamp-offset features and staleness-simulating data augmentation that restore detection performance under misalignment [Fan et al. (Zoox), CVPR 2025 Precognition Workshop]. This literature is the nearest neighbor to ours in spirit: it recognizes that different sensors deliver information at different times. But it treats misalignment as a *fusion-accuracy* problem solved in software, measured by downstream task performance, with per-sensor delays typically estimated from datasheets or software timestamps rather than measured at the wire. It neither isolates the physical-onset-to-first-output term per sensor nor asks *why* that term differs by modality. Our work addresses the complementary, upstream question: the wire-level magnitude and mechanism of each sensor's sense-stage delay, as a verification property of the individual sensor rather than a nuisance to be compensated at fusion.

### Measurement perturbation and external instrumentation

A measurement-science principle motivates our method choice. Software instrumentation perturbs the system it measures, introducing systematic — not merely random — bias that does not average out with repetition and may be undetectable from within the system [Mytkowicz et al., ASPLOS 2009]. For timing claims that must hold for an unmodified deployed system, this is disqualifying: an instrumented measurement characterizes the instrumented system, not the deployed one. The implication is to move the observation point outside the software, to a hardware boundary observed by an independent instrument. Our prior work established this wire-level approach for embedded ML timing — GPIO-edge timestamping with a logic analyzer, including per-platform GPIO-overhead calibration and the finding that timing observability can itself fail under load [Swami & Sonawane (LSENS); Swami & Chougule, arXiv:2605.02835, arXiv:2605.17701]. The present work builds directly on that method; the method is not its contribution.

### The gap: the sense-stage term and its modality-dependent mechanism

Across these literatures a consistent omission appears. Analytical end-to-end models begin at the chain head and assume instantaneous sensing. Edge-AI benchmarks begin at inference and measure compute. Fusion-staleness work estimates per-sensor delay in software and compensates downstream. None measures, at the wire, the interval from physical onset to a sensor's first output — and, more pointedly, none asks whether that interval arises from the *same* cause across sensors or from *different* ones.

Our prior single-sensor work hinted at the stakes: an inertial measurement unit's on-sensor classifier delivered decisions only on an internal window-cadence clock, imposing a sub-second stimulus-to-decision floor invisible to interrupt-referenced measurement [LSENS]. This paper generalizes that observation into a cross-sensor claim. We measure the sense-stage term wire-level, against a servo-generated ground-truth onset, across three modalities chosen to span cadence regimes — an inertial MLC, a pulsed-coherent radar presence detector, and a multizone time-of-flight ranger — and show that the term is not a single phenomenon scaled by output rate but arises from **mechanistically distinct sources**: cadence quantization fixed by an internal update clock (IMU MLC), multi-frame detector settling beyond a single frame period (PCR radar), and ranging-period quantization coupled to multizone readout transfer (ToF). This mechanism distinction — that the omitted sense-stage term must be characterized per modality because its origin differs — is the contribution. It is what conventional functional, inference-timing, and fusion-staleness verification does not capture, and what a sensor-agnostic latency budget systematically mis-estimates.
